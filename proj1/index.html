Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Collection

Reassembling color photographs from a century-old set of red, green, and blue glass-plate exposures — by teaching a computer to slide the three layers back into register.

By Yuxiang Liu · UC Berkeley, Fall 2026

Method Single-Scale Pyramid Results My Picks Bells & Whistles When It Breaks
Overview

Three plates, one photograph

Between 1907 and 1915, Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii travelled the Russian Empire photographing everything through red, green, and blue filters — three separate exposures onto one tall glass plate, meant to be recombined into color long before color film existed.

The Library of Congress digitized the surviving plates. Each scan is a vertical strip holding the three brightness records stacked top-to-bottom in blue, green, red order. Dropping them straight onto the R/G/B channels of a digital image gives a badly ghosted picture, because the camera shifted slightly between the three exposures. The whole job is to find, for each of the green and red plates, the (dy, dx) shift that snaps it back on top of the blue reference — then stack the aligned plates into one RGB image.

01

Split the strip

Cut the scan into three equal horizontal thirds — the B, G, and R exposures.

02

Score a shift

Slide a plate by (dy, dx) and score the overlap with normalized cross-correlation, ignoring the noisy border.

03

Search coarse → fine

An image pyramid finds big shifts on a tiny copy, then refines them level by level on the full plate.

04

Stack & trim

Roll G and R by their best shifts, stack into RGB, and crop the ragged edges away.

The matching metric

Candidate shifts are ranked by normalized cross-correlation (NCC) — each plate is mean-subtracted and normalized to a unit vector, and the score is their dot product. Unlike a raw L2 difference, NCC compares structure rather than absolute brightness, so it doesn't get fooled when one filter simply let in more light than another.

Before scoring, I crop 15% off every side. The plate edges are full of black scanner borders and the fringe where the three exposures don't overlap — matching on those would reward aligning the junk instead of the picture.

Part 1

Single-scale exhaustive search

For the small JPEG plates, the shift is only a handful of pixels, so a brute-force search over a [−15, 15] window in both directions is enough. Every candidate shift is scored with NCC and the best one wins.

Each label reads (dy, dx) — vertical then horizontal shift applied to that plate. The red plate consistently needs about twice the shift of green, which makes sense: red sits farthest from blue on the original strip, so it drifted the most between exposures.

Part 2

Coarse-to-fine pyramid alignment

The full-resolution .tif plates are ~9,000 px tall, and their true shifts run to well over a hundred pixels — far outside any window a single brute-force pass could afford. A recursive image pyramid solves it: estimate the shift on a heavily downsampled copy, double it, then refine within a small window at each finer level.

My picks

Three plates of my own

Pulled from the Library of Congress collection and run through the same pyramid pipeline. One of them, family, forced an interesting choice about what to align on — and is discussed further in When It Breaks.

Bells & whistles

Two things that made it work

Beyond plain translation search, two design choices did most of the heavy lifting on the hard plates.

Aligning on gradients, not pixels

Match edges, not brightness

The emir plate is the textbook failure of raw-pixel alignment: his brilliant blue robe is bright in the blue exposure but nearly black in red, so matching on intensity pulls the plates apart. Instead of the raw plates, I align their gradient magnitude (a Sobel edge map). Edges fall in the same place regardless of which filter was brightest, so the match keys on shared structure — collars, seams, the doorframe — and the emir snaps into register. It's the default for every plate but one: on the carpet-heavy family scene the repeating edges backfire, which is its own story in When It Breaks.

Border handling

Ignore the junk, then trim it

Borders hurt twice. During scoring, the black scanner frame and the non-overlapping fringe are the highest-contrast thing in the plate, so I crop 15% off each side before computing NCC — otherwise the search happily aligns the borders instead of the photo. After compositing, the outer 8% is trimmed from the finished RGB image so the coloured fringe left by the shifts doesn't frame every picture.

Honesty

When it breaks

All seventeen plates end up correctly registered — but one of them only after switching the thing I aligned on, and a couple carry limits that no shift can repair.

Why family is the one plate I don't align on gradients

Gradient features rescue most hard plates, but they sabotage this one. Aligning family's edge maps sends the red plate to (+37, +571) — a false match: the scene is wall-to-wall carpet with dense, near-periodic diamond patterns, and in the edge map those repeats correlate strongly at many horizontal offsets. The coarse pyramid level locks onto the wrong repeat, and the finer levels only polish a shift that was already hundreds of pixels off.

Feeding the raw plates to the same pyramid fixes it: the smooth tonal variation across the carpets is far less self-similar than their edges, so red settles at (+86, −43) — in line with green's (+47, −12) and visibly correct. It's a clean illustration that gradient features are a tool, not a default: they win when brightness disagrees between filters (see emir), and lose when structure repeats.

The residual colour fringing on the standing man survives either way. He shifted between the three exposures, so no single global translation can bring his edges into register — that's subject motion, a limitation of the capture, not the algorithm.

The colour cast on church

Alignment only decides geometry. The three plates were exposed and digitized with different sensitivities, so even a perfectly registered stack can lean toward one hue. A per-channel white-balance or histogram stretch — an optional extension I didn't implement — would neutralize the magenta without touching the shifts.
